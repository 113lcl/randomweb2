Designing for the eleven minutes of gold light nobody asked for

Nicolas Verane
March 2026

No client has ever asked us for eleven minutes of gold light across an entry hall floor, once a year, on the morning of the winter solstice. It isn't a line item. It doesn't fit in a brief. And yet it's the single detail owners mention most often, years after we've handed over the keys.

Every house we design gets a solar study before it gets a single wall. We plot the sun's path across every day of the year, not just the equinox renders most studios stop at, and we look for the moments the site offers that nobody thought to request. A shaft of light that only lands on the kitchen island in late August. A reflection off a pool that climbs the bedroom ceiling for twenty minutes at dusk in June.

The brief is the floor, not the ceiling

A brief tells you what a house needs to do — how many bedrooms, what views to protect, where the cars go. It rarely tells you what a house needs to feel like at seven in the morning, half-awake, coffee not yet made. That gap is where the actual design work happens.

“We don't design the eleven minutes. We just refuse to build anything that would block them.”

At Villa Obsidian, that meant orienting the entry hall two degrees off the axis the topography suggested — a change so small the structural engineer initially flagged it as a drafting error. It wasn't. Those two degrees are the difference between light that grazes the floor and light that simply misses it.

You can't render your way to it

Photorealistic renders are useful for material and proportion, but they lie about light — they show you noon on a clear day, forever. We build physical models and photograph them outdoors, at the actual latitude, across real seasons, before we commit a design. It's slower. It's also the only way to know if the eleven minutes are real.

None of this is unique to expensive houses — it's available to anyone willing to spend a season watching a site before drawing on it. Most of the industry doesn't, because it's difficult to bill for watching. We've found it's the highest-leverage hour we spend on any project.
