Building privacy into a house before anyone asks for it

Elena Voss
November 2025

Every client eventually asks the same question, usually around the third meeting, once trust has settled in: "how do we keep this private?" By then we've usually already answered it — three months earlier, in the site plan, long before anyone typed the word into a brief.

Privacy in architecture isn't a wall or a gate. Those are admissions of failure, a house that couldn't solve the problem with geometry and had to solve it with height instead. Real privacy is designed into the approach road, the sightlines from a neighbouring ridge, the angle a drone would need to see over a roofline that was never meant to be seen from above.

The approach matters more than the gate

We route driveways to reveal a house slowly, or not at all until the final turn — not for drama, but because a visible destination invites a visible audience. At Villa Obsidian, the drive climbs for six hundred metres through existing pine before the house appears, by which point you're already standing at the front door.

“If you need a six-metre wall, the site plan already failed once.”

Digital privacy gets the same early treatment. Smart-home networks are segmented and air-gapped from guest wifi by default; security footage is stored locally, never cloud-synced, unless a client explicitly requests otherwise in writing.

Privacy from the inside, too

The quieter version of this problem is privacy between people who live in the same house. Bedroom wings are acoustically isolated from living spaces by design, not by retrofit — a decision made in the structural drawings, when moving a wall still costs nothing.
